Manuscript I

Before there was fashion
, there was land.

In the highlands of Ethiopia,

where humankind itself first stood upright and walked, wool grows fine enough to remember where it came from. This is not a metaphor. This is where everything begins — not in a boardroom, not in a mood board, but in soil that has watched the first men take their first steps. It is said the first cloth was never woven by human hands at all. It was given. Wool, taken from the sheep, placed by the hand of God upon the first man — not as fashion, but as mercy. Covering, in a world that had only just learned it was bare. Everything since has been a continuation of that hand.

So the world kept giving. In Mongolia, where winters carve character into everything that survives them, the cashmere goat grew a coat so fine it became a second skin for the humans who found it — the closest thing to warmth the earth has ever produced. In Sweden, wool met precision, and what was once just fabric became engineering — fibers refined until they changed what cloth could do. In Bangladesh and China, silk was perfected over centuries into something closer to light than to thread, spun by hands that treated a single strand like an inheritance. Four lands. Four disciplines. One house that refused to choose only one.

Presence
is a choice.

Manuscript II

On Character

ADISS did not invent luxury.
It gathered it.

And gathering was only the beginning — because material alone is not yet a garment. It needed hands. Three of them.

It is never performed for an audience. It is simply how one chooses to live.One hand harvests the cotton, low to the earth, patient as the seasons that grow it. One hand tends the wool and the cashmere, learning the animal before it ever learns the cloth. And the third — the third is where the story turns from origin into craft. That third hand belongs to a room in Paris that time has agreed not to touch. No machine has ever been permitted past its door. Here, a seam is not stitched — it is negotiated, between a needle and a hundred years of instruction passed silently from one master to the next, hand to hand, the way scripture once was passed. The cotton has already remembered the earth it grew from. The wool has already learned the animal that wore it before we did. Now comes the hand that must decide what they become — the cut that flatters without announcing itself, the drape that moves like breath, the silhouette that says everything by insisting on nothing.

A French tailor does not chase trend. He distrusts it. He has watched a hundred seasons arrive loudly and leave quietly, and he has learned that the only garment worth making is the one that will still be correct in fifty years — worn by a man not yet born, admired by eyes that will never know our name.

Character
outlives style.

Manuscript III

Craft & Permanence

Not made for a season,
but for generations.

True luxury is not in what is new, but in what endures. Craft is the bridge between the hands that create and the lives that follow. It is a commitment to quality, to detail, and to a standard that cannot be rushed.

Every seam is a decision. Every decision, a promise quietly kept.

Timeless
by design.

Manuscript IV

The Greater Whole

Beyond what we make,
there is what we leave behind.

A house is measured not by its seasons, but by what it leaves to those who come after. Every thread, every choice and every quiet standard becomes part of a longer story.

To build well is to think beyond oneself — to add to a whole that will outlast the hands that made it.

A legacy in
every detail.
